About me

I am currently an MS student in Electrical Engineering at Binghamton University, concentrating in Physical Electronics and Electro-Optics, with a bachelor's in Electrical Engineering from SUNY Polytechnic Institute. Two threads run through almost everything I have worked on so far, and they have turned out to be the same thread.

The first is analog and power electronics. I like circuits where the answer is not obvious until you do the math. In my graduate analog coursework I designed a CMOS cascode amplifier with an active current-source load that reached 86 dB of gain, working the transconductance and output resistance out by hand from SPICE Level-1 parameters before opening the simulator, then checking that the AC sweep agreed. I did the same on an 8th-order Sallen-Key band-pass filter, solving each biquad's pole locations analytically to confirm stability rather than trusting the plot. That habit of hand analysis first, simulation second is the part of my training I value most.

The second is optics and photonics, which is my graduate concentration. I have modeled directly modulated semiconductor lasers using coupled carrier and photon rate equations with gain compression, solving for threshold current and watching relaxation oscillations develop when the drive current steps. I have worked through fiber-optic link design end to end: coupling efficiency into multimode fiber, polarization through Malus's law, electro-optic modulation, dispersion budgets, and photodiode response. Before that, my bachelor's thesis covered optical processing and photonic integrated circuits as a route past the limits of electronic interconnect.

Those two threads meet at the laser diode driver, which is where I am spending my own time right now. A laser diode is an unforgiving load, since it degrades quickly under current overshoot, so driving one properly is a circuit problem governed by device physics. I started with a straightforward LM317 constant-current LED driver in KiCad because it is simple enough to actually get fabricated and measured, and the line-regulation sweep it needs is the same measurement the laser version will need. From there the design moves toward a precision op-amp and MOSFET current source with proper protection and modulation.

I have also spent real time on the embedded and systems side. During a summer undergraduate research program I built the motor-drive subsystem of an autonomous personal carrier robot, running an L298N H-bridge from a dual-battery supply so motor switching noise stayed off the logic rail, with PID closed-loop control and YOLO-based person tracking on a Raspberry Pi. Afterward, as a research associate, I worked on AI robotics on the NVIDIA Isaac stack and profiled GPU workloads to find inference bottlenecks, which turned out to be as much a power and thermal question as a throughput one. That is part of why power electronics pulled my attention in the first place.

What I want next is a role where I can close the loop between simulation and hardware: design a board, get it made, measure what actually comes back, and write down honestly whether it met spec. I am a U.S. citizen, so cleared and ITAR-restricted work is open to me.
